# Turning Off a Gene for Life — and the One Safety Question a Stranger Can Re-Derive

*A shear study on VERVE-102 (Verve Therapeutics / Eli Lilly) for heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia. Angle: a SEAL. Lifecycle: standing charter, molecular court dark. The subject under grading is the safety **instrument** — never the medicine, the company, or a patient.*

## A disease you are born already carrying

Heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia is not rare in the way most genetic diseases are rare. Somewhere between one in 250 and one in 313 people carry it — usually a single broken copy of *LDLR*, *APOB*, or *PCSK9* — which makes it one of the most common serious inherited conditions in medicine. What it does is quiet and cumulative: LDL cholesterol runs high from birth, and the arteries age under that load for decades before anyone thinks to look. Untreated, HeFH carries something like a ten- to twentyfold higher risk of premature coronary disease — heart trouble arriving in the 40s and 50s rather than the 70s.

The hopeful half is real and worth saying first. This is one of the most *treatable* high-risk conditions there is: with recognition and enough LDL lowering, a person's cardiovascular risk can be pulled back down toward the general population's. The problem was never that treatment fails. It is that only about 15–20% of people with HeFH are ever formally diagnosed, and among those who are, taking a pill every day for the rest of your life is genuinely hard. That adherence gap is exactly the space a one-time treatment is built to close.

## The breakthrough — one infusion, one letter

VERVE-102 is an *in vivo* base-editing medicine. A lipid nanoparticle carries two things into liver cells — messenger RNA for an adenine base editor, and a guide RNA — given as a single intravenous infusion. The editor makes one permanent DNA change and then is gone. It edits hepatocytes only; the germline is untouched.

The mechanism is elegant in how little it changes. The guide points the editor at the splice-donor site where exon 1 meets intron 1 of *PCSK9*. It installs a single A•T → G•C swap there. That one letter breaks normal splicing, intron 1 is retained, a premature stop codon appears, and functional PCSK9 protein stops being made. Less PCSK9 means the liver keeps more LDL receptors on its surface, and LDL falls. It is the deliberate genetic mimic of the natural *PCSK9* loss-of-function variants that already give some people lifelong low cholesterol and low heart-disease risk.

The number in the name matters. VERVE-102 uses the *same editor and guide* as the earlier VERVE-101, but a different lipid nanoparticle — a new ionizable lipid plus a GalNAc ligand, so the particle can enter hepatocytes through the ASGP receptor as well as the LDL receptor. HeFH patients often have reduced LDL-receptor function, the very door VERVE-101 leaned on. Hold onto this: the redesign was a chemistry-and-delivery change, made to fix a chemistry-and-immune problem, not an off-target-sequence one.

The clinical picture, dated honestly. It is investigational — Phase 1b Heart-2 (NCT06164730), FDA Fast Track, no approval, no label, no boxed warning yet. Lilly agreed to acquire Verve in June 2025 (~$1B upfront, up to ~$1.3B with a contingent value right), with a Phase 2 planned after the interim read. The April 14, 2025 *topline* was a press release: in the 0.6 mg/kg cohort of four, a mean 53% and maximum 69% LDL-C reduction, well tolerated. The fuller interim — the peer-reviewed Heart-2 paper (NEJMoa2601283), presented at EAS in Athens around May 2026 — covered 35 participants across six ascending doses: PCSK9 down 51% to 88%, LDL-C down 9% to 62%, and at the top 1.0 mg/kg dose roughly 88% PCSK9 and 62% LDL-C, durable through a year in the 15 with extended follow-up. A single 3 mg/kg dose in non-human primates had anchored this at ~80% PCSK9, ~62% LDL-C. For the record: the 2025 event was a press-release topline and the peer-reviewed paper is 2026 — two datapoints with different sample sizes, not one, and not forced to reconcile.

Safety, as observed: no treatment-related serious adverse events in Heart-2, low-grade infusion reactions in about a fifth, transient non-dose-limiting ALT rises, no clinically significant platelet changes at the doses reported. And the single most important safety fact in the whole file lives one generation back. In Heart-1, VERVE-101 caused a grade 3 transient ALT elevation and a grade 3 thrombocytopenia serious adverse event within days of dosing — resolved, no bleeding — and a further drug-related event with liver-enzyme and platelet effects led Verve to halt Heart-1 enrollment and move to VERVE-102. That is where the program actually bent. It points straight at the risks the exact instrument does *not* touch.

## The one question that is genuinely exact

There is a safety-relevant question about a genome editor that is not a model at all — it is a count. Besides its intended target, where else in the genome does the guide match well enough to risk an edit? Phrase it precisely: enumerate every genomic locus within an edit/Hamming distance *K* of the 20-nt protospacer that also carries a PAM from a declared set — canonical NGG, plus any near-cognate PAMs the editor is known to use. That set — call it the off-target lattice — is finite and exhaustively enumerable once *K* and the PAM set are fixed. The PAM test is a present-or-absent membership test, not a score; a neighbor that carries no PAM from the declared set is *cleanly excluded*, not filed under "low probability." Which adenines fall inside the editor's window is likewise a discrete, countable fact. Tools like Cas-OFFinder already compute this deterministically, with no cap on mismatch count and with bulge (edit-distance) support.

On this sub-problem the exact instrument is the right and better tool — not a fallback. The field's standard is a floating-point similarity score, CFD- or MIT-style, a product of per-position penalty weights. Those are fine for ranking, but the weight tables are *not standardized* — CFD, MIT, and CRISPOR publish different ones — so the verdict on a borderline locus moves with the table you round to, and a regulator who does not hold your table cannot re-derive it. An integer mismatch-count-plus-PAM verdict is byte-identical on every machine, needs no weights, and can be checked by a stranger years later.

This is the same exact-versus-float shear the fusion court measured out of domain. On the observer axis (Study 34), a reactor density-limit verdict computed in floating point with a rounded π contradicts itself on all 142 operating points across four published machine geometries (ITER, SPARC, JET, and DIII-D), while a fixed-rational invariant returns one identical answer. On the time axis (Study 35), a float32 running integrator goes deaf at 2²⁴ ≈ 16.8M updates and float64 at 2⁵³, both proven one line each, while the exact Int128 state runs true past 2¹²⁶. For the sequence case directly, the in-repo demo `reproduce/crispr-guide-offtarget-exact-vs-float.swift` runs on synthetic, representative loci and shows the *kind* of divergence: the exact rule (≤3 mismatches + PAM) flags a PAM-proximal off-target (`off_3mm_proximal`, scores 0.042/0.036) that both float tables miss; the two float tables disagree with each other on the mid-window borderline site (`off_3mm_mid`, table A calls it, table B clears it); the no-PAM neighbor is cleanly excluded. Marker: `CRISPR_OFFTARGET_EXACT_IS_OBSERVER_INVARIANT`. Notice the shape of Verve's own preclinical work — nominate a discrete candidate set with ONE-seq and Digenome-seq, then deep-sequence it in human hepatocytes (they reported no detected off-target editing across the nominated sites). The seal is the re-derivable, observer-invariant version of the *nominate* step.

## Where the exact tool goes silent

Honesty is the credibility, so the boundary is drawn as loudly as the claim. The lattice covers guide-dependent DNA off-targets — and nothing else. It does not reach guide-*independent* deaminase editing of transiently single-stranded DNA, which no guide sequence predicts and which needs wet-lab assays like CHANGE-seq-BE. It does not reach transcriptome-wide RNA off-target editing, which is not a genomic-locus set at all. It tells you *which* adenines sit in the window but not the *rate* at which each is edited, nor the *rate* at a near-cognate PAM — which PAMs to admit into the declared set is a biological input, and how active the editor is at each is chemistry — nor the bystander behavior of the engineered deaminase. And it is entirely silent on the column that actually reshaped this program: LNP tolerability, infusion reactions, complement, immunogenicity, the transient ALT and platelet effects, pharmacokinetics — and permanence itself, since an irreversible edit cannot be dosed back down. That last point is a reason the *design-stage* seal is worth more, not something the seal computes.

The verdict has to stay balanced in both directions. Undersell would be to call the lattice trivial — it is a permanent-genome-edit safety question, and re-derivability is exactly what a regulator lacks today. Oversell would be to imply a green lattice means a safe drug: VERVE-101's off-target sequence work was clean, and its nanoparticle still put patients in the hospital.

## (a) What the substrate can honestly say about this drug

Not a verdict on VERVE-102. No off-target computation was run on its real protospacer and PAM here — the molecular court for the actual sequence is dark, a charter, not a result, and inventing a number would be fabrication. What the substrate can defend is narrower and truer than a whole-drug claim: one necessary leg of the safety case — the guide-dependent off-target lattice — is a discrete, exact, observer-invariant set that a safety authority could re-derive byte-for-byte, without trusting the sponsor, before the first patient is dosed. It is silent on the leg that caused Verve's clinical iteration. Both halves are the honest statement.

## (b) Whether better options exist

Yes, and fairness requires naming them. High-intensity statins plus ezetimibe are the foundation, with decades of outcomes data. The PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies — evolocumab, alirocumab — add roughly 50–60% LDL-C lowering on a statin (about 61% in ODYSSEY LONG-TERM) and, crucially, carry cardiovascular *outcomes* data (FOURIER, ODYSSEY OUTCOMES), at the cost of dosing every two to four weeks. Inclisiran, an siRNA, gives ~47–50% twice a year. Bempedoic acid and evinacumab round out the field. So VERVE-102's LDL magnitude is *comparable to, not larger than*, the approved antibodies (~50–62% vs ~50–60%). Its distinct proposition is one-and-done durability and the removal of the adherence problem — the real Achilles' heel of lifelong therapy. What it does not yet have is what the antibodies do: cardiovascular event-reduction data. The trade is durability and adherence against reversibility and a longer outcomes track record. A genuine choice, not a rout.

## The value, named: a SEAL at the design stage

The angle is a SEAL, not a VETO — VERVE-102 is a working drug with positive, durable Phase 1b data, not an off-envelope design. The seal's worth is *when* and *by whom* it can be computed: before the first patient, and by the safety authority rather than the supplier. For a pipeline drug it turns "nominate candidate off-target sites" from a proprietary wet-lab list into a public, exact set anyone can reproduce — an auditable design-stage screen, cheap against what a late off-target failure burns in lives and lost R&D. That is engineering on a demonstrated principle, applied to one leg of the case, and it says so.

## From day one — what the substrate would have found

Picture the counterfactual, and grade it plainly at the door: this is an ARGUMENT — nothing here was computed on VERVE-102's real protospacer. Suppose the exact instrument had sat in the loop from the first day the guide was drawn, pointed at the same goal Verve was pointed at: a safe, permanent, life-changing edit of *PCSK9*.

On the actual path, the off-target question got the field's best answer — heuristic nomination tools (ONE-seq, Digenome-seq) proposing a candidate set, then wet-lab deep sequencing in human hepatocytes to check them, with the definitive read arriving only after cells, then primates, then patients had been dosed. Careful work, and it came back clean. But it is a proprietary shortlist, produced by the sponsor, readable in full only by the sponsor, re-derivable by no one else — and it arrived late in the story rather than at its beginning.

Run the same first day through the exact instrument and the output is different in kind. Not a ranked list to spot-check in the lab, but the *whole* guide-dependent off-target map, standing before an animal or a patient is ever touched: every genomic locus within the stated edit/Hamming distance of the 20-nt protospacer carrying a PAM from the declared set, enumerated once, byte-identical on any machine, checkable by a regulator who holds no weight table and trusts no sponsor. The near-cognate-PAM neighbors named. The no-PAM neighbors *cleanly excluded* rather than filed under "low probability." A screen a stranger could reproduce in full, years later, from the reference alone.

Is that a research problem or a defined engineering step? The measured demo already shows the divergence is real on representative loci — the exact rule flags a PAM-proximal site both float tables miss, and the two float tables disagree with each other at the borderline. And the scale is not a hope. Affine.Earth already hosts and re-derivably grades molecular libraries at the 10⁵–10⁶ scale — 258,616 PDB structures and 125,302 protein/material aggregates on the live court ([Study 14](Study-14-Protein-Lattice-Manifold)). An exact off-target enumeration against a pinned reference transcriptome or genome is the same class of bounded, deterministic, re-derivable computation, at a scale the substrate has already demonstrated. Ingesting a pinned reference is a defined step, not a research problem.

And here is the honest, hopeful line, bounded as loudly as it is drawn: this would have handed the *guide-dependent DNA* leg of the safety case to the public on day one — at design time, for the price of a computation, with no cells, no primates, and no waiting. It would have changed nothing about the leg that actually bent this program: the nanoparticle, the ALT and platelet signals, the guide-independent and RNA edits, permanence. Those live in chemistry and immunology and pharmacology, and the exact instrument stays silent on all of them. One leg, computed early, computed by anyone — which is exactly why naming it precisely is worth more than overclaiming it. That a single necessary question in a permanent-edit medicine can now be settled in the open, before the first patient, is a real piece of the future arriving.

>
> **We do not claim** that this seal certifies VERVE-102 as safe. No off-target number was computed on its real guide — the molecular court for the actual sequence is dark, a charter, not a result. The risks that actually reshaped the program — LNP tolerability, infusion reactions, transient ALT and platelet effects, guide-independent DNA editing, RNA off-target editing, bystander rate, near-cognate-PAM editing rate, immunogenicity, permanence — are chemistry, immune, and pharmacology; they are not discrete and the exact method does not reach them. A clean lattice is not a safe drug, just as the fusion court is not solved fusion. The edge word on anything unbound is: *not known.*
